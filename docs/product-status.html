What Revenant is

Revenant is an autonomous outbound engineer. A founder points it at their startup; it researches a real prospect, builds and deploys a working prototype personalised to that prospect, films an AI-narrated walkthrough video of it, writes a pitch deck and a personal email, and puts the whole package in front of the founder for approval.

The pitch is not a description of the product — it is the product, already built for you. That is the differentiator, and everything below serves it.

The core belief driving the engineering: an AI that says "done" is not evidence that anything works. Throughout development the dominant failure was the system reporting success while the artifact was actually broken — a dead link, a silent video, a demo that didn't respond. The product now independently verifies its own output before showing it to a founder. That verification is the feature, not a technicality.

The founder's journey

1. Onboard — founder points Revenant at their repo / website. It reads the product and learns what it actually does. 2. Hunt — proposes 3 real, named companies that would genuinely buy it, each with a reason and a named decision-maker. 3. Build — founder picks one. Revenant builds a live prototype using that prospect's real workflow, films a walkthrough, writes deck + email. 4. Verify — before the founder sees it, the work is auto-graded. A failure is reported plainly, not hidden. 5. Approve — founder reviews and sends. Revenant never sends anything itself.

Run any single stage on its own

Founders don't always want the full pipeline. Each specialist can now be invoked independently — "just build the prototype", "film it", "draft the outreach", "QA that campaign again" — without triggering the rest. This was added directly in response to review feedback that stages weren't separately addressable.

Multi-customer readiness

The product now supports multiple startups as genuinely separate customers. Each gets its own isolated workspace: its own campaigns, its own learned product knowledge, its own preferences. Two customers on the same deployment cannot see or overwrite each other's work.

On learned preferences — why this is safe to ship. A system that "learns what the customer wants" can very easily invent preferences nobody expressed, then apply them forever. Revenant will only record a preference if it can quote the founder's own words verbatim from the conversation. If it cannot produce that quote, the preference is discarded and the founder is told it was discarded. It cannot make things up about a customer.

The one thing to fix before any real customer touches this: prototype links are served through a temporary developer tunnel. They work, and the product correctly detects when one has died — but they are not durable. A prospect opening a link a day later may find nothing. Permanent hosting is a prerequisite for launch, not a nice-to-have.

Suggested priorities

  • 1 · Permanent hosting. The only item genuinely blocking customer use.
  • 2 · Decide on enrichment spend. Determines whether emails go to verified contacts and whether the video sounds human. Directly affects reply rates.
  • 3 · Turn on the hard access boundary. Cheap to enable; do it before customer #2 exists, not after.
  • 4 · Publish a quality benchmark. The grading engine exists; run it across a sample so we can state a real pass rate rather than an anecdote.
  • 5 · Self-repair on failure. Groundwork is in place; converts an honest failure into a silent recovery.

How to judge it yourself

The quality bar is measurable, not a matter of taste. Every campaign is checked on: is the prototype link actually alive; does the interactive demo actually respond; does the video contain real audio and real video; is the deck complete; does the email reference genuine evidence about that prospect rather than generic filler.

A campaign that fails any of these is reported as a failure to the founder, with the specific reason. The product is designed to tell you bad news.
